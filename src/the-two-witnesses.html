The Two Witnesses

"I will commission my two witnesses to prophesy for those twelve hundred and sixty days, wearing sackcloth" (Revelation 11:3). I propose who they may be: the prophet Elijah, and Pope Leo XIV.

Home › The Possibility › The Two Witnesses

Where this page standsThis is Fr. John's proposal, not the Church's teaching. The Church has never defined who the two witnesses are. I set no date and begin no count of days (CCC 673). The text says the witnesses are killed; I do not expect, predict or wish the death of anyone, and I ask every reader to pray for the Holy Father's health and long life. The Holy Father has said nothing of this reading and bears no responsibility for it.

The text

God's two witnesses prophesy for 1,260 days, wearing sackcloth. They are "the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth" (Revelation 11:4). Fire comes from their mouths against those who would harm them; they have power to close the sky and to strike the earth with plagues (11:5–6). "When they have finished their testimony, the beast that comes up from the abyss will wage war against them and conquer them and kill them" (11:7). Their bodies lie in the street of the great city, "where indeed their Lord was crucified," while the world rejoices (11:8–10). After three and a half days the breath of life from God enters them, and they go up to heaven in a cloud as their enemies look on (11:11–12).

The text gives four things to look for:

  • Two who testify together, for a measured time, in sackcloth, the dress of penance.
  • Signs: fire, drought, plagues.
  • The beast's war on them, ending in their death in the great city.
  • God's answer: they are raised and taken up.

How the Church's writers have read it

  • Elijah's return is the firmest thread. "Now I am sending to you Elijah the prophet, before the day of the LORD comes" (Malachi 3:23). He is "destined... to re-establish the tribes of Israel" (Sirach 48:10). The Lord himself said, "Elijah will indeed come and restore all things," while teaching that John the Baptist had already come in Elijah's spirit (Matthew 17:11–13). St. Augustine calls Elijah's coming before the judgment "a familiar theme in the conversation and heart of the faithful," and expects that through his teaching "the Jews shall believe in the true Christ" (City of God XX.29).
  • The second witness is less settled. The Haydock commentary calls it "a very common interpretation" that the two are Enoch and Elijah, but notes that the Fathers do not agree on Enoch: St. Hilary reports that some expected Jeremiah, and himself expected Moses (on Apocalypse 11).
  • Others read the two as the Church herself. St. Cyprian took them as two kinds of martyrs. Others, Haydock records, took them as the testimonies of the Old and the New Testament. St. Bede reads them chiefly as the Church, made one out of two peoples, and the two olive trees as the two Testaments that feed her lamp; he also records the reading of Enoch and Elijah (Explanation of the Apocalypse).
  • Peter and Paul. Among modern scholars who tie the passage to St. John's own day, the leading identification has been the apostles Peter and Paul, martyred in Rome; the Jesuit Juan de Mariana proposed it long ago, and Johannes Munck argued it in 1950, taking the "great city" to be Rome and noting that the text does not confine the witnesses' ministry to that city (surveyed in Ian R. Brown, The Two Witnesses of Revelation 11:1–13, Andrews University, 2016, pp. 329–330, 337–338). It is a minority reading, and Brown, a Protestant scholar, does not adopt it; but it shows that seeing Peter among the witnesses is not new.
  • The Catechism does not name the witnesses. It teaches that the Messiah's coming awaits his recognition by "all Israel" (CCC 674; Romans 11:25–26), and that the Church will follow her Lord through a final trial into his death and resurrection (CCC 675, 677).

So the tradition offers two kinds of reading: two men, of whom Elijah is nearly always one; or the Church's own witness, drawn from both Testaments. My proposal joins them.

My reading

I read the witnesses as two individuals who stand for the two Testaments. Elijah stands for the Law and the Prophets and comes for the sake of Israel, as Malachi and Sirach promise. For the second I depart from the common opinion. Where many name Enoch, I propose that the witness who stands for the New Testament and the Church is the one who holds Peter's office when the beast makes war: in our time, Pope Leo XIV. The two lampstands recall that in this book a lampstand is a church (Revelation 1:20), and the word "witness" is in Greek the word "martyr." If scholars have seen Peter himself among the witnesses, it is a short step to ask whether Peter's successor may be asked to bear the same witness at the end.

A grade on this page answers one question: if the two witnesses are given in our time, who are they? Whether their testimony has been seen is a separate question, answered under "Standing." For both, almost nothing has yet been seen.

1. The prophet Elijah

The fit. The witnesses' signs are Elijah's own. He closed the sky for three years and six months (1 Kings 17:1; James 5:17), the same span as the 1,260 days, and fire fell at his word (2 Kings 1:10). He did not die but was taken up (2 Kings 2:11), which is why the Fathers held that he is kept for a work still to come. Scripture promises his return by name, and gives its purpose: to turn hearts, and to restore Israel.

What he stands for. Elijah comes as the prophet of the God of Israel to the people to whom "belong the covenants" and whose gifts and call are irrevocable (Romans 9:4; 11:29). His mission is the reconciliation St. Paul foresaw, which the Church awaits in hope and does not schedule (CCC 674). Nothing in it is a judgment on the Jewish people; it is a promise made to them.

The case against.

  • The Lord said that in John the Baptist "Elijah has already come" (Matthew 17:12). Some conclude that Malachi's promise is fulfilled and no personal return remains.
  • Bede and others read the witnesses as the Church's whole witness, not as two men.
  • The other signs, plagues and water turned to blood, are those of Moses, and some Fathers expected Moses, not Elijah alone.
  • Elijah has not appeared. Until he does, this half of the reading is an expectation received from the tradition, not something seen.

2. Pope Leo XIV

My reading. If the first beast is the man I propose (see also The Antichrist, as First Written), then the one man on earth who has borne public witness against his works in Christ's name, and whom he has answered by name, is the Pope. No one in today's world more clearly stands for the Church than the successor of Peter, and the tension between him and the man I propose as the first beast is on the public record. I see in Leo XIV the beginning of a testimony, and I do not see its end. If the witnesses are given in our time, I grade it probable that he is the second.

A testimony, given in public.

  • When the United States and Israel went to war with Iran in 2026, he spoke against it from the first. On Palm Sunday he said that God does not listen to those who wage war, quoting Isaiah 1:15. On April 11, at a vigil for peace in St. Peter's, he said, "Too many innocent people are being killed," and spoke of a "delusion of omnipotence" (Al Jazeera). In June he said of that war, "There is no just war there" (NPR).
  • He has defended the migrant and backed the American bishops who did so (The First Beast, card 7), and has warned that machines "are tools, not persons" (The Second Beast, card 10).

The beast's answer.

  • On April 12, 2026, the president wrote that the Pope was "weak on crime" and "terrible for foreign policy," and that same day an image of himself in the likeness of Christ appeared on his account (Al Jazeera; The First Beast, card 5).
  • The next day the Pope said he had "no fear of the Trump administration," and that his appeals for peace come from the Gospel (CNN). Days later, in Cameroon, he said the world was "being ravaged by a handful of tyrants" (Wikipedia, citing the BBC and the Guardian).
  • He is the first pope born in the United States, the nation the first beast governs. A witness from within the beast's own house would be a mercy to that nation. I note it and build nothing on it.

The case against.

  • No Father, Doctor or approved commentator names a pope as one of the two witnesses. The tradition's second witness is Enoch, or Moses, or Jeremiah. This is my proposal alone, and "probable" is my own judgment of it: three of the text's four tests are not met.
  • Every pope bears witness, and popes have withstood rulers before: Pius VII and Napoleon, Pius XI and Hitler, John Paul II and the Soviet empire. None of those rulers was the beast, and none of those popes one of the two witnesses. Leo's words against a war do not single him out.
  • He has said, "I do not look at my role as being political," and that he does not wish to debate the president. Relations have had calmer seasons. A quarrel between a pope and a president is not the beast's war on the witnesses.
  • The witnesses work signs: fire, drought, plagues. He has worked none.
  • The two testify together for the same 1,260 days. The text gives them, as one commentator puts it, "the same task, the same duration for their work, the same authority, the same miraculous power," so that "it is impossible to allot anything to them individually"; another observes that "the two witnesses always appear together" (Gerhard Maier and Ekkehardt Müller, quoted in Brown, pp. 325–326). I had hoped to find support for two witnesses who work apart and meet only in death. The text does not give it. At most it leaves open where each one labors, since their ministry is not confined to the great city. Elijah has not come, so by the text's own terms the testimony of the two has not begun, and no count of days can be started from anything Leo has said.
  • The witnesses die in the city "where indeed their Lord was crucified," which is Jerusalem. The Pope lives in Rome. Haydock records that some read the great city as Rome or as any city that persecutes, and Munck read it as Rome; those are readings, and the plain sense points to Jerusalem.
  • This half of the proposal depends on my reading of the first beast. If that is wrong, this falls with it.
  • To name a living pope as a witness who is to be killed could breed a morbid watchfulness or worse. I reject that. The witness is given in life, the time belongs to God, and my duty and yours is to pray for him.

Would strengthen: the appearing of Elijah, or of one who does his promised work in Israel; the first beast turning from words to force against the Pope or the Church's worship; signs given through the witnesses. Would weaken: peace between the Holy See and the first beast; the end of this pontificate or of that presidency with none of this seen. Standing: not yet seen.

Fatima and the bishop in white

In the third part of the secret of Fatima, published by the Holy See in 2000, the three children saw "a Bishop dressed in White," of whom they said, "we had the impression that it was the Holy Father." He passes "through a big city half in ruins," praying for the dead he meets, climbs a mountain to a great Cross, and there "he was killed by a group of soldiers who fired bullets and arrows at him," and after him bishops, priests, religious and lay people (The Message of Fatima).

The likeness to Revelation 11 is real: a witness in white, a ruined city, death at the hands of armed power, beneath the Cross. I think it fair and helpful to set the two side by side. I also keep to what the Church has said about the vision:

  • Cardinal Ratzinger's commentary, published with the text, says that "the future is not in fact unchangeably set, and the image which the children saw is in no way a film preview of a future in which nothing can be changed." Its purpose is "to mobilize the forces of change in the right direction." He adds: "Insofar as individual events are described, they belong to the past," and he notes that St. John Paul II, after the attempt on his life on May 13, 1981, saw in the vision his own fate (same document).
  • Ten years later, as Pope, he said at Fatima: "We would be mistaken to think that Fatima's prophetic mission is complete" (Benedict XVI, homily, May 13, 2010).

I hold the two together in this way. Fatima does not tell us that a particular pope will be killed, and I do not read it as a forecast about Leo XIV. It does tell us that the Church's road passes by the Cross, that a pope may be asked to walk at the head of his people on it, and that prayer and penance can change what is to come. That is the same lesson as the two witnesses, and it is why the first duty this page asks of anyone is prayer for the Pope. See Mary and the Saints.

Almost nothing of this mark has been seen. I set it out because, if the first beast is here, the witnesses cannot be far behind him in the story Revelation tells, and the faithful should know what the text says of them: that the beast is allowed to conquer them only "when they have finished their testimony," and that God's answer to their death is resurrection. The witnesses are not victims of the beast. They are the proof that he does not have the last word.

What is asked of us is plain whoever the witnesses prove to be: to bear our own witness, to do penance, and to pray daily for the Pope. See Stand Firm and Prayers and Consecration.
