Revelation 12:5

The Male Child

The dragon waits to devour the child, and he cannot. In one short verse, Saint John tells the whole story of Jesus, and it ends in victory.

“She gave birth to a son, a male child, destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod. Her child was caught up to God and his throne.”Revelation 12:5

In the verse before this one, the scene is tense. A huge red dragon stands before the woman, “about to give birth, to devour her child when she gave birth” (Revelation 12:4). We hold our breath. Then the child is born, and before the dragon can touch him, he is with God. That is the whole drama of verse 5, and it is good news from beginning to end.

A son, a male child

Who is this child? Saint John gives us the answer in the words he chooses. The child is “destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod.” That is the language of Psalm 2, the psalm of the Lord’s anointed king: “With an iron rod you will shepherd them” (Psalm 2:9). Later in Revelation the same words are said of the rider on the white horse: “He will rule them with an iron rod” (Revelation 19:15). The NABRE note on 12:5 points to both passages. The child is the Messiah. The child is Christ.

The doubled phrase, “a son, a male child,” echoes the prophet Isaiah, who saw Zion giving birth in a wonderful way: “Before her pangs come upon her, she delivers a male child” (Isaiah 66:7). The people of God had waited for this birth for centuries. Now it has come.

We know the story of that birth from the infancy narratives in the Gospels (Matthew 1:1–2:23; Luke 1:5–2:52). There is no dragon on the surface of those pages, but look closely and you will see him. A jealous king hears of a newborn king and sends soldiers to Bethlehem (Matthew 2:16). The child is carried off to Egypt in the night. The dragon was waiting at the manger.

He came in humility

Notice how the Savior chose to come. Not with an army, not with a crown, but as a baby, poor and small, born to a young mother far from home. He shared our life completely. The Letter to the Hebrews says we have “one who has similarly been tested in every way, yet without sin” (Hebrews 4:15). He was like us in every way except sin.

This matters for us. The King who will rule the nations did not come to frighten us. He came to be close to us. He knows hunger, weariness, grief and temptation from the inside. When we bring him our weakness, we are not bringing it to a stranger.

Caught up to God and his throne

Here is the surprising thing about this verse. Between “she gave birth” and “caught up to God,” Saint John says nothing at all. No preaching, no miracles, no cross. The whole earthly life of Jesus is folded into a single breath. Why?

Because Saint John wants us to see the end of the story. His short life was victorious. He completed the mission of salvation that the Father gave him. The NABRE note on 12:5 says that “was caught up to God” refers to Christ’s ascension. The dragon wanted to devour the child at his birth. He tried again at the cross. He failed both times. The tomb was empty, and the risen Lord went up to his Father.

The Catechism teaches what this means. Christ’s Ascension “marks the definitive entrance of Jesus’ humanity into God’s heavenly domain” (CCC 665). And his being seated at the Father’s right hand “signifies the inauguration of the Messiah’s kingdom” (CCC 664). The child of Revelation 12 is not only safe. He reigns. Saint Paul calls him “the King of kings and Lord of lords” (1 Timothy 6:15), and Revelation gives the Lamb this title as well (Revelation 17:14; 19:16). He reigns now, and he will reign forever.

What the Fathers saw

The Fathers of the Church loved this verse, and they saw more than one thing in it. St. Hippolytus of Rome saw the woman as the Church, “always bringing forth Christ, the perfect man-child of God” (On Christ and Antichrist 61). For him the child’s being caught up to the throne shows that “he who is always born of her is a heavenly king, and not an earthly.”

St. Methodius of Olympus went a step further. He saw the Church giving birth to Christ in the hearts of believers, so that “the Church labours and gives birth to those who are baptized” (Banquet of the Ten Virgins, Discourse 8.7). In other words, what happened to the Head happens to the members. Christ is born, and in Baptism we are born again in him.

These readings do not compete with the plain meaning. The child is Christ first of all. But where Christ goes, he takes his people. The Catechism says that Jesus, the head of the Church, goes before us into the Father’s glorious kingdom, “so that we, the members of his Body, may live in the hope of one day being with him for ever” (CCC 666).

The victory is already won

So here is what I want you to take from this verse. The dragon is real, and he is hungry. He waited for the child, and he waits for us. Saint Peter warns that the devil prowls “looking for [someone] to devour” (1 Peter 5:8). But the dragon could not devour the child, and he cannot devour those who belong to the child.

The battle that matters most has already been fought. Jesus said so the night before he died: “In the world you will have trouble, but take courage, I have conquered the world” (John 16:33). We are not waiting to find out who wins. We already know. Our task is to stay close to him, to pray, to receive the sacraments, and to trust.

When you read the darker pages of Revelation, come back to this verse. Before the dragon makes war on the woman and her children, Saint John has already shown us the child on the throne. That is where the story goes. That is where we are going too.
